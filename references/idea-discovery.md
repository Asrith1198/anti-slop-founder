# Idea Discovery

Use this when the person doesn't have a locked idea yet — they want to *find* one, not validate one they already have. The goal is not "generate 50 random startup ideas" (low-value, generic) — it's narrowing from their actual situation to a small number of ideas worth taking into validation.

Don't skip straight to brainstorming product ideas. Ideas generated with no grounding in the founder's actual edge, actual market signals, or an actual observed problem are the weakest kind — they're the ones that sound fine in a chat and fall apart on contact with reality. Work through the steps in order.

## Step 1 — Map their edge

Ask (batch into one round, 2-3 questions, options where possible — see the Intake section in SKILL.md):
- What do they already know more about than most people — a skill, an industry, a community they're embedded in, a problem they've personally lived with?
- What do they have access to that most people starting a venture wouldn't — a specific audience, a distribution channel, existing relationships, technical ability, capital, an existing brand/venture that could feed this one?
- Time and resource reality — is this a weekend/side project, a full build, do they have runway, a team?

The point: an idea that uses an existing edge is defensible from day one. An idea with no connection to the founder's actual position is competing on pure execution speed against everyone else who had the same generic idea.

## Step 2 — Read market signals

Before brainstorming specific products, get a read on where attention and capital are currently moving — this doesn't pick the idea, but it tells you whether a direction is swimming with or against the current.

Search for, as relevant to the sector(s) under consideration:
- Recent funding rounds in the space (last 3-6 months) — are investors actively writing checks here, or has it gone quiet?
- Y Combinator's current public "Requests for Startups" and recent YC batch launches in adjacent categories.
- Trending Product Hunt launches and categories over the last few months.
- Platforms like Razorpay's "Fix My Itch" (an India-focused, AI-structured bank of real user-submitted problems, scored by severity/frequency/market whitespace/TAM) for pre-surfaced, evidence-backed problem statements rather than guessing from scratch.
- Sector-specific news (regulatory changes, new platform APIs, new payment rails, demographic shifts) that just opened a door that wasn't open a year ago — timing unlocks are some of the best idea sources, since "why now" is often the real differentiator between an idea that failed in 2015 and the same idea succeeding today.

Summarize this back as a short signal readout, not a data dump: which 1-3 directions look genuinely active right now, and which look crowded/cooling.

## Step 3 — Mine for real problems, not invented ones

Techniques, roughly in order of reliability (most-grounded first):

1. **Their own friction.** What has the founder personally been annoyed by, worked around with a spreadsheet, or complained about to a friend in the last month? Self-sourced problems come pre-validated for at least one real user (them).
2. **Observed friction in their community.** What do people in their actual network (classmates, colleagues, local community) complain about repeatedly? Ask them to list 5-10 specific complaints they've personally heard more than once — not hypothetical ones.
3. **Unbundling a clunky incumbent.** A widely-used but widely-disliked existing tool/process is a reliable idea source — the complaints are already public (reviews, forum threads) and the market already proven to exist, it's a question of serving it better or narrower.
4. **Forum/review mining.** Scan review sites and complaint-heavy forums for the sector (see idea-validation.md's source list — the same sources used for validation are useful here too, just read earlier/more broadly before a specific idea is picked).
5. **Adjacent-market arbitrage.** A solution that already works well in one market/geography/demographic and hasn't been brought to another — lower invention risk since the concept is proven, the work is localization/adaptation.
6. **Timing unlocks.** A new API, regulation, platform shift, or price collapse (compute, hardware, a new distribution channel) that makes something newly possible or newly cheap that wasn't before.

Generate wide here — aim for at least 8-12 raw problem candidates before narrowing, not 2-3. Quantity at this stage costs nothing and the best candidate is rarely the first one that comes to mind.

## Step 4 — Score and converge

Score each candidate problem (not yet a full product idea — just the problem) on:

| Axis | Question |
|---|---|
| Founder-fit | Does this use their edge from Step 1? |
| Problem severity | Is this a real pain, or just a minor annoyance people tolerate? |
| Market size | Roughly how many people/businesses have this problem? (ballpark, directional) |
| Competitive intensity | How crowded is this already — and if crowded, is there a defensible wedge? |
| Timing | Why is now a better time to attack this than 3 years ago, or 3 years from now? |
| Feasibility | Can a first version get built and tested with the resources from Step 1? |

A candidate that scores well on founder-fit and timing but is unproven on size/severity is exactly what Step 5 and the full idea-validation.md process are for — don't demand certainty here, just enough signal to justify spending real validation effort on 1-3 finalists instead of all 10.

## Step 5 — Decide and articulate

Narrow to 1 (or at most 2-3 to validate in parallel) candidate problems. For each finalist, write a one-line articulation in the form:

> "[Specific audience] struggles with [specific problem] because [specific reason existing options fail them]."

This one-liner is the handoff point — it's exactly the input the Brand Identity workflow's intake needs ("what it is" + "who it's for"), and it's the thesis that idea-validation.md will stress-test with real research next.
