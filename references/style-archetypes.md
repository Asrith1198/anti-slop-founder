# Style Archetypes

Twelve distinct "worlds" a brand can live in. Each has a name, the feeling it produces, reference brands (for orientation, never to copy), and when it's the right call. Cross-reference the same archetype name in color-palettes.md, typography.md, and logo-concepts.md to keep a system coherent.

---

### 1. Corporate Systematic
**Feel:** trustworthy, structured, built to scale across 50 products and 20 countries.
**References:** Google, Microsoft, IBM, SAP.
**Signals:** grid-based logo construction, one primary brand color + a disciplined secondary palette, geometric humanist sans, lots of internal consistency rules (this is a *system*, not a single logo).
**Use for:** B2B software, enterprise tools, anything that needs to look credible to a procurement department, multi-product parent companies (e.g. an umbrella entity that many sub-brands sit under).
**Trap to avoid:** boring-by-default. Corporate doesn't have to mean lifeless — Google's mark is playful within a rigid system; that tension is the point.

### 2. AI-Native Minimal
**Feel:** quiet confidence, restraint, "we don't need to shout."
**References:** Anthropic/Claude, OpenAI, Perplexity, Notion.
**Signals:** warm off-white or true black backgrounds (rarely bright white), a single accent color used sparingly, lowercase or sentence-case wordmarks, a serif/sans mix or a humanist sans with unusual warmth, generous negative space, almost no gradients.
**Use for:** AI products, research tools, anything positioning as thoughtful/serious rather than hyped.
**Trap to avoid:** the purple-blue gradient blob and the circuit-board/neural-net icon. This archetype became a cliché *because* everyone copied its surface (gradients, glass) without the restraint that made the originals work. Restraint IS the differentiator — don't gradient it up.

### 3. Quirky-Technical / Indie Dev
**Feel:** made by people who actually use it, opinionated, a little dry-witted.
**References:** Kiro, Linear, Raycast, Arc Browser, Vercel.
**Signals:** a small, precise geometric mark (often abstract, sometimes literally a simple shape rendered perfectly), monospace touches in UI/copy even if not in the wordmark, one saturated accent on a dark or near-black base, personality shows up in microcopy and easter eggs more than in loud visuals.
**Use for:** developer tools, productivity software, anything for a technical/early-adopter audience that would be turned off by corporate polish.
**Trap to avoid:** trying too hard to be quirky in the mark itself — the wit lives in the *product/copy*, the logo itself is usually understated and precise.

### 4. Streetwear / Mascot Rebel
**Feel:** loud, funny, a little transgressive, tribal (you either get it or you don't).
**References:** The Souled Store's devil mascot, Supreme, Thrasher, Stüssy.
**Signals:** a character or mascot logo (devil, skull, cartoon face, animal), high-contrast palette (often black/white plus one loud color), slab serif or graffiti-adjacent display type, the mark works as a standalone sticker/patch.
**Use for:** apparel, youth/campus brands, anything that wants cult status over broad appeal.
**Trap to avoid:** a mascot with no specific personality — "generic edgy skull #4127." The mascot needs a name, an attitude, a specific expression, the way the devil in Souled Store's mark is grinning and self-aware, not just menacing.

### 5. Stylish Performance-Tech
**Feel:** engineered, aggressive, premium-but-not-polite.
**References:** Nvidia, Razer, high-end gaming/automotive brands.
**Signals:** near-black backgrounds, one bold accent (acid green, gold, or a saturated brand color used like a warning light), angular/chevron geometry, a heavier-weight condensed or geometric sans, motion-implying shapes (diagonals, arrows, cut corners).
**Use for:** performance hardware, gaming, anything selling power/speed/precision as the emotional payload.
**Trap to avoid:** overdoing the aggression until it reads generic-esports. The best version of this archetype is confident, not shouty — one sharp gesture, not fifteen.

### 6. Playful Edtech / Student
**Feel:** friendly, energetic, approachable to a 16-year-old and their parent both.
**References:** Duolingo, BYJU'S, Khan Academy.
**Signals:** bright, high-saturation multi-color palette (not just one accent — 3-4 colors doing real work), a rounded friendly mascot or icon set, rounded sans typography, illustration over photography.
**Use for:** student-facing products, community/campus brands, learning platforms — good default territory for ventures aimed at students.
**Trap to avoid:** cute-but-empty. The mascot/illustration needs a point of view (Duo the owl is unsettling on purpose), not just "rounded and smiling."

### 7. Warm Food & Hospitality
**Feel:** appetite, warmth, "made by someone who cares," local.
**References:** independent cafés, regional cloud-kitchen brands, Zomato's illustrative work.
**Signals:** warm palette (red/orange/mustard/terracotta), organic or hand-touched shapes, a script or humanist serif accent alongside a clean sans for legibility on menus, texture (paper, stamp, hand-drawn line) over flat vector polish.
**Use for:** food brands, cloud kitchens, hospitality, anything selling a sensory/comfort experience.
**Trap to avoid:** stock "foodie" clichés — chef hats, generic fork-and-knife icons, Comic-Sans-adjacent "fun" script fonts. Specificity (a regional ingredient, a real dish, a real texture) beats generic food iconography every time.

### 8. Heritage / Craft
**Feel:** rooted, trustworthy through time rather than through scale, story-first.
**References:** modernized traditional/artisanal brands, heritage textile and food houses.
**Signals:** earthy, muted palette, a serif or a script/display face with cultural roots (can be a modernized Devanagari-inspired treatment for Indian-rooted brands), a stamp/seal/badge logo construction, texture and imperfection as a feature.
**Use for:** brands leaning into cultural roots, regional identity (e.g. Telugu/Andhra craft, heritage food, legacy reinvention), anything where "this has history" is the selling point.
**Trap to avoid:** costume-y nostalgia with no modern edit — heritage cues need one contemporary counterweight (a clean modern sans for body copy, a restrained palette) or it reads dated rather than rooted.

### 9. Civic / Hackathon Energy
**Feel:** urgent, collective, "build something that matters, fast."
**References:** civic-tech and hackathon-circuit brands, badge/shield-based event identities.
**Signals:** one bold primary color + black/white, a badge, shield, or flag-derived mark, condensed or bold display type for energy and legibility at a distance (banners, stage screens), works well at both huge (stage backdrop) and tiny (favicon) scale.
**Use for:** hackathons, civic/community initiatives, event brands, anything that needs to read as movement rather than company.
**Trap to avoid:** looking like a generic government seal or a generic startup logo — lean into the *specific* cause/theme in the mark, not a stock shield.

### 10. Fintech Trust
**Feel:** safe with your money, but not boring — competent and modern.
**References:** Stripe, Razorpay, Paytm.
**Signals:** navy/blue-dominant palette with exactly one energetic accent color, clean geometric sans, an abstract (not literal) mark — avoid literal coins/wallets/rupee-symbol clichés, precise grid-based construction similar to Corporate Systematic but warmer and less institutional.
**Use for:** payments, banking-adjacent, anything handling money or sensitive transactions where trust is the primary emotional job.
**Trap to avoid:** the literal money icon (₹ symbol, coin stack, wallet). Abstract geometry reads as more sophisticated and ages better.

### 11. Brutalist / Indie Maximalist
**Feel:** raw, unpolished on purpose, anti-corporate as a statement.
**References:** indie creative studios, zine/art-collective brands, anti-design-as-design movements.
**Signals:** oversized type doing double duty as the logo itself, harsh black/white base with one clashing (often "wrong") accent color, deliberately unrefined layout choices, little to no gradient/shadow/polish.
**Use for:** creative agencies, art/culture brands, anything positioning against the slickness of "startup design."
**Trap to avoid:** brutalism done timidly just looks like an unfinished corporate brand. It has to be a full, confident commitment — half-brutalist reads as a mistake, not a choice.

### 12. Luxury Minimal
**Feel:** expensive by omission — what's left out is the flex.
**References:** Apple-adjacent premium tech, high-end product brands.
**Signals:** monochrome base (black/white/one neutral) plus a single metallic or deeply saturated accent used rarely, thin-to-regular weight type (serif or geometric sans), the mark reduced to its most essential possible form, massive negative space.
**Use for:** premium/flagship products, anything where the price point itself is part of the brand promise.
**Trap to avoid:** minimal-but-empty. Every remaining element has to be doing more work, not less — there's no room for a mediocre typeface choice when there's nothing else on the page to distract from it.

---

## Choosing between archetypes for a given brief

Ask: who's the audience, and what do they need to feel in the first two seconds?
- Need to feel *safe/credible* → Corporate Systematic, Fintech Trust, Heritage/Craft
- Need to feel *smart/ahead of the curve* → AI-Native Minimal, Quirky-Technical, Luxury Minimal
- Need to feel *hyped/energized* → Streetwear/Mascot Rebel, Stylish Performance-Tech, Civic/Hackathon Energy
- Need to feel *welcomed/included* → Playful Edtech, Warm Food & Hospitality
- Need to feel *this is different from everything else in the category* → Brutalist/Indie Maximalist, or a deliberate two-archetype hybrid

Multi-brand parent entities (an umbrella company running several independent-feeling sub-brands) can and should assign a *different* archetype to each sub-brand rather than forcing one visual system across all of them — that's normal and often the right call when each sub-brand targets a different audience.
